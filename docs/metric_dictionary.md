# Financial Metric Dictionary

This document explains the main metrics used in the Corporate Financial Health Dashboard. The dashboard uses SEC Company Facts data, and because SEC tags vary across companies and industries, not every metric is available for every ticker.

## Revenue

Revenue measures the total sales or operating income generated by a company during a fiscal period. For retailers and software companies, revenue is usually a direct measure of business scale. For banks and insurers, revenue can be reported differently because their business models depend on interest income, premiums, investment income, and financial assets.

## Revenue Growth

Revenue growth measures how much revenue increased or decreased compared with the prior year.

Revenue Growth = Current Year Revenue / Prior Year Revenue - 1

Positive revenue growth usually means the business is expanding, but it should be interpreted alongside margins and cash flow.

## Operating Income

Operating income measures profit from core operations before certain non-operating items. It helps show whether the company can generate earnings from its main business activities.

## Operating Margin

Operating margin measures operating income as a percentage of revenue.

Operating Margin = Operating Income / Revenue

Higher operating margin usually means the company keeps more profit from each dollar of revenue. Operating margin is useful across many industries, but comparisons are strongest within the same industry.

## Operating Cash Flow

Operating cash flow measures cash generated from normal business operations. It can differ from net income because accounting profit includes non-cash items and timing differences.

## Capital Expenditures

Capital expenditures measure cash spent on property, equipment, technology infrastructure, stores, warehouses, and other long-term assets. Capital expenditure needs vary heavily by industry. Retailers and manufacturers often have large capital expenditures, while software companies may have lower capital intensity.

## Free Cash Flow

Free cash flow estimates how much cash remains after operating cash flow is used to fund capital expenditures.

Free Cash Flow = Operating Cash Flow - Capital Expenditures

Positive free cash flow can support reinvestment, debt repayment, acquisitions, dividends, or share repurchases.

## Free Cash Flow Margin

Free cash flow margin measures free cash flow relative to operating cash flow.

Free Cash Flow Margin = Free Cash Flow / Operating Cash Flow

This shows how much operating cash remains after capital expenditures.

## Days Sales Outstanding

Days Sales Outstanding, or DSO, estimates how long it takes a company to collect cash from customers.

DSO = Average Accounts Receivable / Revenue * 365

DSO is useful when accounts receivable is meaningful. For retailers with many cash or card transactions, DSO can be very low. For banks, insurers, and some software companies, DSO may be unavailable or less meaningful depending on reporting.

## Days Inventory Outstanding

Days Inventory Outstanding, or DIO, estimates how long inventory stays on hand before being sold.

DIO = Average Inventory / Cost of Revenue * 365

DIO is most useful for inventory-heavy businesses such as retailers, wholesalers, manufacturers, and consumer goods companies. DIO is usually not meaningful for banks, insurers, and many software companies.

## Days Payables Outstanding

Days Payables Outstanding, or DPO, estimates how long a company takes to pay suppliers.

DPO = Average Accounts Payable / Cost of Revenue * 365

DPO is useful for companies with meaningful supplier payables and cost of revenue.

## Cash Conversion Cycle

Cash Conversion Cycle, or CCC, estimates how long cash is tied up in working capital.

CCC = DSO + DIO - DPO

A lower CCC usually means a company converts spending into cash more quickly. CCC is most useful for inventory-heavy operating businesses. It should not be forced onto banks, insurers, or companies that do not report inventory and cost-of-revenue metrics in a compatible way.

## Revenue Forecast

The dashboard uses simple baseline forecasting models. Current models are meant to be interpretable, not precise predictions. Forecasts should be treated as directional analysis for learning and comparison, not as investment advice.

## Scenario Analysis

Scenario analysis estimates how financial outputs may change under different assumptions. Examples include downside revenue growth, base case revenue growth, upside revenue growth, operating margin changes, and free cash flow changes. Scenario assumptions are illustrative and are not company guidance.

## Industry Applicability

| Metric | Retail | Software | Banks | Insurers |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Revenue | Strong | Strong | Useful with context | Useful with context |
| Revenue Growth | Strong | Strong | Useful with context | Useful with context |
| Operating Margin | Strong | Strong | Useful with context | Useful with context |
| Free Cash Flow | Strong | Strong | Sometimes limited | Sometimes limited |
| DSO | Sometimes useful | Useful for subscription or enterprise sales | Usually not ideal | Usually not ideal |
| DIO | Strong | Usually not useful | Not useful | Not useful |
| DPO | Strong | Sometimes useful | Usually not ideal | Usually not ideal |
| CCC | Strong | Usually limited | Not appropriate | Not appropriate |

## Important Limitation

Different companies use different SEC XBRL tags. A missing metric does not always mean the company does not have that concept. It may mean the company reports it differently, uses a different tag, or operates in an industry where the metric is not appropriate.
